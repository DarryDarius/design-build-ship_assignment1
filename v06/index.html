Science of the Cold

Wind Chill, Not the Thermometer, Is What Bites

A Zero-Degree Morning With a 15 m.p.h. Breeze Feels Like −19° on Bare Skin

The number on your phone’s weather app is the air temperature. The number that matters is the wind chill: how cold it feels on exposed skin once the wind strips away the thin blanket of warm air your body makes.

In Chicago, wind chills below zero are routine in January, and readings of −30°F or lower are not rare. During the polar vortex of January 2019, the air fell to −23°F and the wind chill approached −50°F.

The National Weather Service computes the figure by formula, printed here for the curious:

WC = 35.74 + 0.6215T
− 35.75V0.16 + 0.4275TV0.16 T = air temperature, °F; V = wind speed, m.p.h. Thus 0°F in a 15 m.p.h. wind gives about −19°F, and 15°F in a 20 m.p.h. wind feels like −2°F.

The practical lesson is simpler: read the wind chill before leaving home, and cover every patch of skin.

By the Gazette Staff · With Reporting From Members Who Survived Last Year

HYDE PARK — The leaves are still on the trees and the lakefront still smells faintly of summer, but veterans of this city’s winters say newcomers have about six weeks to get ready. The first snow usually falls in November. By January, the average high is 31°F and the average low is 18°F — figures that, to a student arriving from Lagos, Mumbai, São Paulo or Jakarta, may read like a misprint.

They are not a misprint, and they are not a catastrophe. “Chicago winter is survivable,” said a spokesperson for the First Winter Club, a student-run peer support group for international students facing their first season of snow. “Millions of people do it every year. The difference is preparation, not toughness.”

The club’s advice begins with one word: layers. A single enormous coat, members say, is the classic first-year mistake. It is too hot on the train, too thin at the bus stop, and miserable once wet. Three thinner layers trap warm air between them and come off indoors.

The Three Layers

Base. A thermal top and leggings of merino wool or synthetic fabric that moves sweat off the skin. Never cotton, which holds moisture and turns cold.

Mid. A fleece or light down jacket — the layer that actually holds heat.

Shell. A windproof, waterproof parka long enough to cover the hips. It stops the wind, which the club calls the real enemy (see Wind Chill, at left).

Then the Edges

A hat pulled over the ears. Mittens rather than gloves, since fingers kept together stay warmer. A scarf or neck gaiter over the face. Insulated, waterproof boots with real grip, for sidewalks that stay icy for weeks.

None of it need be expensive (see Gear, below), and none of it need be figured out alone. The club meets Thursdays at 6 P.M. on campus, runs a gear swap in November, and hands every member a free First Winter Checklist: one page on what to buy, what to skip, and what to do on the coldest days.

“Nobody where I grew up owns a hat,” one member said. “Now I own four. I’m fine. You will be too.”

Light & Mood

Sun to Set at 4:20 P.M.; Lunchtime Light Prescribed

Shortest Day Brings 9 Hours, 8 Minutes of Daylight. Members Urge Newcomers to Look After Each Other.

On Dec. 21, the shortest day of the year, Chicago gets about nine hours and eight minutes of daylight, and the sun sets around 4:20 P.M. — often before the last seminar lets out.

Short, gray days can wear on anyone’s mood, and especially on students raised near the equator. Members recommend getting outdoors, or at least beside a big window, in the middle of the day; asking a doctor whether a vitamin D supplement makes sense; and keeping a standing plan with friends so that no one spends a January week indoors alone.

“Check in on each other,” the club says, “is the most important line on the checklist.”

Consumer Report

Warmth Need Not Cost a Fortune

Thrift Racks, Warehouse Aisles and a Free Club Swap Can Outfit a Newcomer for Far Less Than Feared

A full winter outfit does not require a luxury parka. Members point first to thrift stores, where wool sweaters, fleeces and sturdy coats turn up all season long, and second to Uniqlo’s HEATTECH line for inexpensive base layers.

Costco is the club’s pick for multipacks of warm socks and thermal basics. And at the club’s own November gear swap, seniors and second-years hand down the parkas, boots and mittens they no longer need — free to members.

The rule of thumb, repeated at every meeting: spend on the shell and the boots, the two pieces that face the wind and the slush. Save on everything else.
