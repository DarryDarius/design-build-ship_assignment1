First Winter Club

a letter, for your first Chicago winter

with a few notes pinned in

Hyde Park, the first week of October

Dear you, arriving this fall,

I don’t know your name yet, or where you’re flying in from. But if it’s somewhere like where I grew up — Chennai, where January means about 84°F and the warmest thing I owned was a school cardigan — then I think I know a little about the winter you’re about to have. Three winters ago it was mine. Nobody wrote me a letter like this, so I’m writing one to you.

The short version, in case you only read this far: a Chicago winter is survivable if you prepare. The longer version is below. Wherever I mention something to wear, I’ve pinned in a little card — what it is, how warm, where to get it cheap. Make some tea first.

· · ·

The first really cold morning came in early January. My phone said 2°F, and I remember thinking, fine, I have a coat. Then I turned the corner and the wind came in off the lake, and the number that actually mattered was the small one underneath: feels like −20°F. My eyelashes stuck together. I couldn’t feel my cheeks for an hour.

feels like · try my first morning, or yours

If the air is °F and the wind off the lake is mph, it feels like

−20°F

Frostbite on bare skin in about 30 minutes. Cover your face.

Or try: · ·

The National Weather Service wind chill formula. It only counts at 50°F and below, with wind over 3 mph.

Here is the thing I wish someone had told me plainly: in Chicago, the wind chill matters more than the temperature. January isn’t usually dramatic — the average high is around 31°F, the average low around 18°F, and most days are just the ordinary kind of cold you can walk through. But the wind turns ordinary cold into something you have to respect. It regularly pushes the feels-like number below zero, and some years well past −30°F. In the polar vortex of January 2019, before my time, the air itself reached −23°F and the wind chill came close to −50°F. People still talk about it the way my grandmother talks about the cyclones.

What I eventually copied onto a sticky note and put by my door:

feels like −18°Ffrostbite on bare skin in about 30 minutes

feels like −35°Fabout 10 minutes

feels like −48°Fabout 5 minutes

Check the feels-like, not the temperature. Cover your face.

It isn’t meant to frighten you. It’s only the number to look at before you leave the house, the way at home you’d glance at the sky for rain.

· · ·

I made the cotton hoodie mistake, which I think everyone from somewhere warm makes exactly once. I wore my thickest cotton hoodie under one enormous coat, because more fabric seemed like more warmth. I walked fast to my nine o’clock, sweated under all of it, and the cotton held onto every drop. By the time I walked home in the dark I was colder than if I’d worn less. Cotton gets damp and stays damp, and damp is cold.

What actually works is three thin things instead of one thick one.

You don’t need to spend much, which surprised me most of all. Add up every card above and nearly all of it is a single $. My first real parka came from a thrift store and cost less than one of my textbooks. My base layers were Uniqlo HEATTECH — two tops and two leggings, so one set could always be in the wash. A friend with a Costco membership took a few of us along one Saturday for wool socks in bulk. And every November the club holds a gear swap, where people who are graduating, or who finally bought the right boots, leave behind the things that got them through. Half of what I wear now once kept someone else warm.

· · ·

The part nobody mentions is the dark. On the shortest day of the year, December 21, the sun sets at around 4:20 in the afternoon, and there are only about nine hours of daylight — nine hours and eight minutes, if you’re counting, and by then you will be. I’d come out of the library at five and it would already be night. For weeks I didn’t understand why I felt so heavy, why I couldn’t call my mother without crying into the phone about her kitchen. It wasn’t only homesickness. It was the light, too.

What helped, slowly: going outside in the middle of the day, even for fifteen minutes, while the sun was properly up. Asking a doctor about vitamin D, instead of guessing. And — this is the big one — letting people check in on me, and learning to check in on them.

· · ·

Thursday nights are what saved me. In my first November, a girl from Lagos I’d met in a lab section asked if I wanted to come to a thing on Thursday at six. It was First Winter Club: a room on campus, a kettle, a pile of mismatched mittens, and a couple of dozen people from Manila and São Paulo and Jakarta and Dubai, all comparing the coldest they had ever been. Someone showed me how to tuck my scarf so the wind couldn’t find my neck. Someone else walked me to the right bus stop. Nobody made me talk if I didn’t want to.

I went every Thursday after that. Some weeks we talked about boots. Most weeks we just sat near the radiator and were warm together, which turned out to be the whole point.

So that is what I wanted to tell you. The winter here is real, and it is survivable — more than survivable, if you get ready for it, and if you don’t do it alone. The first snow usually comes in November. When it does, please go outside and look up. It is the most beautiful thing, and you will have earned it.

Warmly,

Meera

third year · still from Chennai
